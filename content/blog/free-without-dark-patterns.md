Every game we've released is free, and none of them contain an in-app purchase. People ask
how that works, so here is the whole model — and what it costs us.

## What the games run on

Ads. Banners, and optional rewarded ads you can choose to watch in exchange for in-game
coins. That's it. There is no premium tier, no remove-ads purchase, no currency pack.

That means the worst thing that can happen to you in one of our games is watching a video.
You will never reach a level that asks for money to continue.

## Why not just add purchases

Once a game can sell you something, the design starts arguing for the sale. Difficulty tends
to spike right where a booster would help. Lives run out exactly when you're enjoying
yourself. None of that has to be deliberate — it's what happens when the fun and the revenue
pull in different directions.

Removing the option removes the argument. When a level in
[Soda Jam](/games/soda-jam-color-sort/) is hard, it's hard because the puzzle is hard. If it
stops being fun, that's a level to fix rather than a moment to monetise.

## No account, and no reason to have one

None of the live games ask you to sign up. There's no profile to create and no email to hand
over, which means there's also no personal data to protect, leak or sell.

A useful side effect: with nothing to validate against a server, the games work offline.
Progress saves to your device first and syncs later if it needs to. Planes, metros,
basements — they all work.

## The trade-offs, honestly

- **Revenue is slower.** Ads on a modest player base do not add up quickly.
- **No large spenders.** Most free-to-play income comes from a small number of people
  spending a lot. That route is closed by choice, so a game has to be worth opening again
  tomorrow rather than worth paying for today.
- **Growth has to come from the games.** No purchase funnel to optimise means the only lever
  is whether people enjoy playing and tell someone.

For a one-person studio, that's a reasonable trade. It keeps the work focused on the part
that matters: whether the puzzle is good.

Each game's [privacy policy](/privacy/) spells out exactly what it does and doesn't collect.
They're short, and written to be read rather than agreed to.
